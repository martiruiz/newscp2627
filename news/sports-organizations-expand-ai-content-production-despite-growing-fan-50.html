Sports organizations expand AI content production despite growing fan backlash

Social News · 27 October 2026 · Source: Front Office Sports

A new survey by Front Office Sports and Teamworks of more than 200 sports industry leaders found that 40% of organizations already use AI for content or media production, with another 24% planning to start within a year. Adoption is higher among pro teams (45%) than in college sports (29%), and around nine in ten respondents expect AI use to grow over the next two to three years.

The push comes amid visible fan resistance. Caitlin Clark criticized the Indiana Fever for an AI-altered Instagram image, Washington State faced criticism over an AI web-content deal with FanWord, and AI graphics at Dodger Stadium and Citi Field drew complaints. Meanwhile, the New Orleans Saints and St. Louis Blues publicly promised their graphics are made by people, not AI.

For social teams, the lesson is to use AI where fans won't notice or mind, such as analysis, planning and audience insights, while keeping fan-facing visuals human-made and carefully checked. Some clubs are even turning a no-AI stance into a brand message. Any AI-assisted asset needs a strict quality review, since a single distorted image of a star player can become the story.

AI 22 October 2026 SponCon Sports

How Barcelona, Liverpool and PSG Turn Google Gemini Deals Into Useful Fan Content

FC Barcelona is running a pre-match Instagram Story series sponsored by Google Gemini. A four-frame sequence opens with a mock Gemini prompt asking for facts about the next opponent, followed by three frames of answers paired with player imagery. The series is part of the Gemini and Pixel partnerships Google announced in August with Barcelona, PSG, Arsenal, Liverpool and Bayern Munich. A SponCon Sports analysis praises it for showing a practical way to use the product rather than simply displaying AI output.

The analysis also notes limits. The slides are text-heavy, and PSG's similar feed carousels averaged 30.8K engagements across three posts reviewed, modest for a club with more than 60M followers. Other activations have done more. Liverpool used Gemini to introduce new signing Bradley Barcola and to quiz fans, and recalled a win over Brentford with linked YouTube clips. Barcelona's October fixture preview passed 482K likes, and its players now ask Gemini a question during YouTube membership Q&As.

SPORTS MARKETING 27 October 2026 Front Office Sports

USC's Jazzy Davidson discusses Venmo NIL campaign and building her personal brand

USC women's basketball standout Jazzy Davidson sat down with Front Office Sports for a video interview covering her partnership with Venmo on its Money Moves campaign and her own habits around managing NIL income. She also talked about her approach to personal branding, being a role model for young fans, playing alongside JuJu Watkins, her pro options across the WNBA, Unrivaled and Project B, and uneven media coverage of women's sports.

For sports social teams, the interview shows how brands are pairing college athletes with practical, values-driven messaging like financial literacy rather than simple product plugs. Athletes who position themselves as role models for kids give sponsors a credible, family-friendly voice, and that kind of story travels well in short video, Q&A and carousel formats.

X 27 October 2026 Front Office Sports

World Wide Wob quits NBA X, citing algorithm shift and weak creator pay

Rob Perez, the NBA personality known as World Wide Wob, has stepped away from his X account of more than one million followers after the Knicks won the title in June. In an interview with Front Office Sports, he pointed to X's move toward a For You recommendation feed, turnover in the platform's sports team, and creator earnings he called uncompetitive versus other platforms, alongside roughly 15 years of nonstop content burnout.

Perez has not disappeared entirely. He streams LA police chases daily on Twitch, posts some NBA content on Threads, where he says he has enjoyed working with the platform's team, and keeps a protected secondary X account for his core community. He compared that approach to YouTubers running alternate channels for content outside their main focus, and did not rule out returning to X someday.
