From one-off clips to series: the episodic shift in sports social media trends

By Martí Ruiz · 19 October 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • One of the clearest sports social media trends of 2026 is clubs publishing short video in episodes, with recurring characters and a story arc.
  • Platforms are building for series. Meta has been testing an episodic Series feature for Reels on Instagram and Facebook since June.
  • The Chicago Cubs released Nine Innings to Love, a 17-episode scripted rom-com published only on TikTok, aimed at the #BookTok and #RomanceTok communities.
  • The Kansas City Chiefs used El Offseason, a nine-episode Spanish-language micro-novela released twice a week, to fill the off-season and reach Spanish-speaking fans.
  • Clubs can start small with a numbered recurring format or a three-episode mini-drama filmed on a phone at their ground.

For years, a club's feed has worked like a stream of single moments: a goal, a signing, a meme, then the next thing. One of the clearest sports social media trends of 2026 breaks that habit. Teams are starting to publish short video the way TV channels publish shows: in episodes, with recurring characters, a story arc and a reason for fans to come back tomorrow. Front Office Sports described it in early October as teams and leagues behaving like year-round entertainment companies, with the Kansas City Chiefs' marketing chief admitting there was no blueprint, so they are building one.

This is not about one viral video but about how the most ambitious accounts now plan: fewer isolated posts, more series. Here is what the leading examples share and how any club, league or brand can test the format.

Why sports social media trends are moving towards series

Three forces push in the same direction. First, the platforms are building for episodes. Meta has been testing a Series feature on Instagram and Facebook since June that lets selected creators group Reels into an episodic collection, with a dedicated tab on the profile, so a viewer who finds one episode in the feed can tap through to the rest. It is still a limited test, but it shows where short video is heading.

Second, the audience for short drama is already there. Digital i's research on the YouTube channels of five microdrama apps found that women aged 45–54 accounted for 15.7% of streams to those channels, against 7.7% of overall YouTube streams. For sports teams trying to reach beyond their core fan base, that is a large audience that does not necessarily watch highlights.

Third, a series solves the hardest problem in a sports social media strategy: retention. A highlight is consumed and forgotten. An episode ends with a question, and the answer is on your profile.

The Cubs: a rom-com built for TikTok

The clearest example of the year comes from baseball. On 4 September, the Chicago Cubs released Nine Innings to Love, a 17-episode scripted romantic comedy made by the club's in-house Cubs Productions, with support from Coyote Sun Productions, and published only on the team's TikTok. The story follows two strangers seated together in the Wrigley Field bleachers over the course of one game. According to Front Office Sports, it was aimed squarely at the #BookTok and #RomanceTok communities. The first episode had 786,000 plays, 12,100 likes and 2,493 shares on TikTok at the time of writing.

Why it works: the stadium is the set, not the subject. The series sells the experience of a day at Wrigley to people who may never search for a baseball clip. That is a lesson worth copying for any of your sports TikTok content ideas: start from the community you want to reach, then find the role your venue or team plays in their world.

The Chiefs: one format, many audiences

The Kansas City Chiefs have been building the same muscle for longer. In January 2024, before a playoff game against the Miami Dolphins, they released Falling for Football, a trailer parodying a Hallmark romance, starring Janel Parrish and Tyler Hynes, with a cameo from Donna Kelce and appearances by Chiefs players. The trailer has more than 561,000 views on the club's YouTube channel.

This year, the club pushed the idea further with El Offseason, its first scripted comedy series, made entirely in Spanish for fans in Mexico, Spain and the US. The nine-episode micro-novela premiered on 12 June and was released twice a week across Instagram, TikTok and Facebook internationally, and on YouTube in the US. It was made with an all Spanish-speaking cast and crew. The first episode has more than 348,000 views on YouTube alone.

Two choices stand out. The series fills the quietest period of the sports content calendar, the off-season, when there are no games to post about. And it uses a fixed release rhythm (Tuesday and Friday), which trains the audience to come back.

What makes an episodic format work

Across these examples, the same building blocks repeat:

  • A simple premise in one line. "Two strangers, one game, one promise" or "four friends with no football to work on". If it needs a paragraph, it is too complex for a feed.
  • Short, vertical episodes. Built for the phone first, so each episode can live as a standalone Reel or TikTok and still pull viewers to the next one.
  • A fixed release rhythm. Twice a week, or the full series in one drop with clear numbering. Both work; improvising does not.
  • The club as the world, not the hero. The stadium, the staff and the fans are the setting.
  • A clear target community. Romance readers, Spanish-speaking fans, families.

How to test it at your club

You do not need 17 episodes to start. These social media ideas for sports teams scale down well:

  1. Turn a recurring format into a numbered series. A weekly kit-man diary or an academy player's season is already a story; give it a title, episode numbers and a fixed day.
  2. Write a three-episode mini-drama set at your ground. One matchday, one storyline, filmed on a phone with staff or local actors. This works just as well for football social media content ideas as for baseball or basketball.
  3. Use the off-season on purpose. Plan a series for the weeks without games and schedule it in your planning tool before the season ends.
  4. Cross-post with intent. Publish each episode as one of your sports reels ideas on Instagram and as a TikTok, and pin episode one.
  5. Build one series for a new audience. A language, a city or a community you want to grow in, the way the Chiefs did with Spanish speakers.
