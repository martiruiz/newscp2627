Social media in sports: why Nike won the World Cup marketing battle after the films were released

By Josep · 22 June 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Nike treated distribution as part of the creative process, while adidas treated it mainly as a publishing process.
  • Of the 32 cast members in Nike's Rip The Script, 24 shared campaign content on their own accounts, creating 38 separate posts.
  • Only two members of the adidas Backyard Legends cast actively promoted the campaign themselves, as adidas relied mostly on Instagram's collaboration feature.
  • Nike's signed Polaroid teaser gave talent a ready-made asset to share, which dramatically increased participation.
  • Nike extended the campaign after launch with follow-up content featuring LeBron James and Serena Williams, and treated creators as content producers rather than amplifiers.

When adidas launched Backyard Legends ahead of the 2026 World Cup, the football marketing world and everyone working in social media in sports paid attention. The film was packed with star power, iconic football figures, and the kind of cinematic storytelling that has defined adidas campaigns for decades. A few weeks later, Nike responded with Rip The Script, reigniting one of the most iconic rivalries in sports marketing.

Most of the conversation focused on creativity. Which film was better? Which story resonated more? Which brand captured the spirit of the World Cup?

But the most important lesson from these campaigns had nothing to do with the films themselves. It was about distribution. More specifically, it was about how Nike treated distribution as part of the creative process, while adidas treated it primarily as a publishing process. For anyone working in social media in sports, this is one of the most valuable campaign case studies of the year.

The real social media in sports competition started after the films launched

Both brands understood a modern reality: A hero film is no longer enough.

Campaigns spread through clips, screenshots, behind-the-scenes content, reactions, creator posts, and social conversations. Both Backyard Legends and Rip The Script were built with this in mind. Each included:

  • Individual talent moments
  • Shareable scenes
  • Memorable cameos
  • Content designed to be clipped and repurposed

The difference emerged after launch. Nike and adidas approached talent activation in completely different ways.

Adidas generated reach, Nike generated participation

On paper, adidas assembled an exceptional cast. The campaign featured football stars and global icons including:

  • Lionel Messi
  • Jude Bellingham
  • Lamine Yamal
  • Pedri
  • Zinedine Zidane
  • David Beckham

However, only two members of the cast actively promoted the campaign on their own channels. Timothée Chalamet shared a teaser and the full film. David Beckham shared the segment in which he appeared. The majority of adidas’ distribution strategy relied on Instagram’s collaboration feature. This ensured content appeared on athletes’ profiles, but the content still originated from adidas.

The result? Visibility without volume. Every piece of content depended on what adidas decided to publish. Nike took a completely different route.

Nike transformed talent into media channels

Of the 32 cast members featured in Rip The Script, 24 shared campaign-related content through their own accounts. That represented approximately 75% activation.

More importantly, those participants created 38 separate posts. The campaign was no longer being distributed by one brand account. It was being distributed by dozens of personalities simultaneously.

That changes everything. Instead of a single speaker, Nike created an ecosystem of voices. Fans discovered the campaign through:

  • Cristiano Ronaldo
  • Kylian Mbappé
  • Erling Haaland
  • LeBron James
  • Kim Kardashian
  • Zlatan Ibrahimović
  • Virgil van Dijk
  • Young Miko

And many more.

Each audience entered the campaign from a different direction. This is one of the most important principles in modern sports marketing: people trust people more than brands.

The genius of the Polaroid strategy

One of Nike’s smartest decisions happened before the campaign was even released. The teaser consisted of signed Polaroids featuring the campaign cast alongside the message: “Time to go off script.”

At first glance, it looked like a simple teaser. In reality, it became a distribution asset. Later, many cast members incorporated those same Polaroids into their behind-the-scenes posts.

Nike had unintentionally solved one of the biggest challenges in influencer and athlete marketing: Participation. The campaign gave talent something easy to share. No additional production. No complicated briefing. No extra creative work. The asset already existed. That simplicity dramatically increased participation rates.

Creators became force multipliers

Nike’s strategy extended beyond athletes and celebrities. The brand also invited football creators into the campaign ecosystem. This is where many sports properties still fall short. They view creators as amplifiers.

Nike treated them as content producers. The results were substantial. Creators generated:

  • Independent storylines
  • Native platform content
  • Behind-the-scenes access
  • Additional reach beyond football audiences

One creator alone generated millions of views from content filmed during the same production day. This demonstrates a major shift happening across social media in sports. The most successful campaigns are no longer built around one hero asset.

They are built around content ecosystems.

The campaign continued after launch

Another underrated aspect of Nike’s strategy was narrative continuity. Most campaigns end once the commercial is released.

Nike extended the story. The company created follow-up content featuring LeBron James and Serena Williams that connected directly to storylines introduced inside the original film. This achieved two things:

Extended attention

The campaign remained relevant after launch week.

Rewarded engaged audiences

Fans who followed the campaign closely understood the references. This transformed the campaign from a film into an evolving narrative. The best campaigns do not end with publication. They create reasons to return.

What sports organizations can learn from this

The lessons extend far beyond Nike and adidas. Any club, federation, league, or content creator can apply these principles.

Design for participation

Do not ask people to create content from scratch. Give them assets they naturally want to share.

Build multiple entry points

Different audiences should discover the campaign through different voices.

Think beyond launch day

Plan follow-up stories before the campaign goes live.

Empower creators

Creators should not simply distribute content. They should create it. These principles increase fan engagement, extend campaign lifespan, and improve audience growth.

Why original content mattered even more in 2026

The timing of Nike’s strategy was especially important. As social platforms increasingly prioritize original content, creator-led distribution becomes more valuable than reposting branded assets. Behind-the-scenes content, personal reactions, and creator perspectives align much more closely with how modern algorithms reward content. Nike’s approach was not only creative. It was platform-native. That distinction matters.
